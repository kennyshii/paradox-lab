01 The paradox

Before you can walk across a room, you have to reach the halfway point. Before you can cover the rest, you have to reach half of what remains: the three-quarter mark. Then seven-eighths, then fifteen-sixteenths, and so on without end. That is infinitely many tasks, and you can’t complete infinitely many tasks one after another. So, Zeno of Elea argued around 450 BC, you can never arrive. In fact, you can never even start. Yet people cross rooms all the time. What’s wrong with the argument?

02 Make a guess

Where does the argument go wrong?

\(\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots = 1\). The infinitely many stages fit inside a finite distance and a finite time, since each stage takes half as long as the one before.

04 Why it works

An infinite sum with a finite total

After \(k\) stages you have covered

$$S_k = \frac12 + \frac14 + \cdots + \frac1{2^k} = 1 - \frac1{2^k}.$$

No finite \(S_k\) reaches 1, but the gap \(2^{-k}\) can be made smaller than any distance you name. That is precisely what it means for the infinite series to converge to 1:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac1{2^k} = \lim_{k\to\infty} \left(1 - \frac1{2^k}\right) = 1.$$

Time shrinks too

Walking at a steady speed, each stage takes half the time of the one before. If crossing takes 10 seconds, the stages take 5 s, 2.5 s, 1.25 s, …, and those add up to 10 s as well. Zeno’s hidden premise is that infinitely many tasks must take infinitely long. That’s true only if each takes at least some fixed minimum time, and these don’t.

Not every infinite sum converges

Shrinking terms aren’t enough on their own. The harmonic series \(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \cdots\) has terms that shrink to zero, yet its partial sums grow without bound (roughly like \(\ln k\)). Zeno’s series converges because its terms shrink geometrically. Tick the comparison box in the lab to see the difference.

Why it’s falsidical

The argument relies on a premise that seems obvious but is false: that a sum of infinitely many positive amounts must be infinite. The rigorous theory of limits, developed in the 19th century by Cauchy and Weierstrass, shows exactly when infinite sums are finite. (Some philosophers argue a deeper puzzle remains, about how one can “complete” an infinite sequence of acts, but the mathematical core is settled.)
