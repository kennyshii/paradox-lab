01 The paradox

A year has 365 days. Gather people in a room one by one: how many do you need before there’s a better-than-even chance that some two of them share a birthday? With 365 possible days, it feels like you’d need a crowd in the hundreds, or at least half of 365. The real answer is small enough that most school classes have a shared birthday hiding in them.

04 Why it works

Count the chance of no match

It’s easier to compute the probability that all \(n\) birthdays are different. The first person can have any birthday; the second must avoid one day, the third must avoid two, and so on:

$$P(\text{no match}) = \frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdot\frac{363}{365}\cdots\frac{365-n+1}{365} = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{365}\right).$$ $$P(\text{at least one match}) = 1 - \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{365}\right).$$

For \(n = 23\) this gives \(1 - 0.4927 \approx 0.507\).

Pairs, not people

Intuition asks “will someone match me?”, which has only \(n-1\) chances. But a match can happen between any two people, and the number of pairs grows quadratically:

$$\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}, \qquad \binom{23}{2} = 253.$$

Each pair matches with probability \(1/365\). Treating pairs as roughly independent gives a handy approximation:

$$P(\text{match}) \approx 1 - e^{-n(n-1)/(2\cdot 365)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n \approx \sqrt{2\cdot 365\,\ln 2} \approx 22.5.$$

In general you need about \(\sqrt{d}\) items, not \(d/2\), before collisions among \(d\) possibilities become likely. That same square root is why cryptographers worry about “birthday attacks” on hash functions.

Why it’s veridical

The calculation is correct and the simulation agrees with it. Real birthdays aren’t perfectly uniform (there are seasonal peaks), but that only makes matches more likely.
