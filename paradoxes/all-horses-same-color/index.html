01 The paradox

Here is a “proof” by mathematical induction that all horses are the same colour. One horse is obviously one colour. Now suppose any group of \(n\) horses is all one colour, and take \(n+1\) horses. Leave out the first one: the remaining \(n\) are one colour. Leave out the last one: those \(n\) are one colour too. The horses in the middle belong to both groups, so the two colours are the same, and all \(n+1\) horses match. Every step looks fine, yet there are plainly brown horses and white horses. What went wrong?

02 Make a guess

Where is the flaw?

The step from 1 horse to 2 horses fails: the two groups of one horse don’t overlap, so nothing links their colours. Every other step is fine, but the chain is broken at its first link.

    04 Why it works

    What induction needs

    To prove a statement \(P(n)\) for every \(n \ge 1\), induction needs two things:

    $$P(1) \quad\text{and}\quad \forall n \ge 1:\; P(n) \Rightarrow P(n+1).$$

    The “for all” is essential. One broken link stops the chain of dominoes.

    The overlap argument

    For a herd of \(n+1\) horses, the proof forms \(A = \{2, \dots, n+1\}\) and \(B = \{1, \dots, n\}\). It then uses a horse in \(A \cap B = \{2, \dots, n\}\) to show the colours agree. That intersection has

    $$\lvert A \cap B \rvert = n - 1$$

    horses. For \(n \ge 2\) it is non-empty and the argument is valid. But for \(n = 1\), going from one horse to two, \(A = \{2\}\), \(B = \{1\}\) and \(A \cap B = \varnothing\). There is no horse to compare.

    $$P(1) \not\Rightarrow P(2).$$

    Why it’s falsidical

    The proof is persuasive because the step is usually valid; it just fails for the one case the whole chain depends on. In fact \(P(2) \Rightarrow P(3) \Rightarrow \cdots\) all hold, so if any two horses were guaranteed to match, all horses would. They aren’t, so the conclusion is false. The puzzle is due to the mathematician George Pólya, as a warning about checking that induction steps hold at the boundary.
