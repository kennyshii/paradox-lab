01 The paradox

You’re on a game show facing three closed doors. Behind one is a car; behind the other two, goats. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the car is, then opens one of the other doors to reveal a goat, and offers you a choice: stick with your original door, or switch to the one remaining closed door. Two doors are left, one car. Surely it’s a coin flip, so does switching make any difference?

02 Make a guess

What should you do?

Switching wins 2/3 of the time; staying wins only 1/3. Try it below, then run a few thousand simulated rounds.

04 Why it works

Your first pick is stuck at 1/3

When you pick, the car is behind your door with probability \(1/3\) and behind one of the other two with probability \(2/3\). The host’s reveal can’t change where the car already is. More importantly, it gives you no information about your door: whatever you picked, he can always find a goat to show you.

$$P(\text{car behind your door} \mid \text{host opens a goat}) = P(\text{car behind your door}) = \tfrac{1}{3}.$$

Since the car must be behind one of the two closed doors, the other one gets everything else:

$$P(\text{win by switching}) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{2}{3}.$$

Switching = taking both other doors

Another way to see it: switching is equivalent to being allowed to open both of the doors you didn’t pick. The host just opened the empty one for you. Two doors beat one.

The 100-door version

With \(n\) doors, the host opens \(n-2\) goats and leaves one other door closed. Your original pick is still right only \(1/n\) of the time, so switching wins

$$P(\text{switch wins}) = \frac{n-1}{n}.$$

With 100 doors, you picked door 37 and the host carefully avoided opening door 82 out of 98 others. Door 82 is now very suspicious: it is right 99% of the time.

The crucial assumption

The answer depends on the host always opening a goat door and always offering the switch. If he opened a random door that just happened to show a goat, it really would be 50/50. That hidden rule is why the puzzle fooled so many people when Marilyn vos Savant published it in 1990, but with the standard rules, the 2/3 answer is simply correct.
