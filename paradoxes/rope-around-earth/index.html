01 The paradox

Wrap a rope snugly around the Earth’s equator, about 40,000 km of it. Now cut it and splice in just one extra metre, then lift the rope evenly all the way round so it hovers above the ground. One metre spread over forty million metres sounds like nothing. How big is the gap: could an ant crawl under it, or a cat? And if you instead grabbed the rope at one point and pulled it straight up until it went taut, how high could you lift it?

02 Make a guess

Lifted evenly, the rope with 1 extra metre floats about…

The gap is \(1/2\pi\) metres, about 15.9 cm, and it is the same for a basketball, the Earth or the Sun. Pulled up at a single point, the same metre lifts the Earth rope about 121 m.

04 Why it works

Lifting evenly

A rope hugging a sphere of radius \(R\) has length \(C = 2\pi R\). Add \(\Delta L\) and lift it evenly to height \(h\): it now forms a circle of radius \(R + h\), so

$$2\pi (R + h) = 2\pi R + \Delta L \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{\Delta L}{2\pi}.$$

\(R\) cancels completely. For \(\Delta L = 1\text{ m}\), \(h = 1/2\pi \approx 0.159\text{ m}\) around a basketball, the Earth, or the Sun. Our intuition compares the extra metre with the circumference (one part in 40 million) and expects a tiny gap. But the gap is measured against the radius, and circumference and radius grow together: every extra metre of radius always costs exactly \(2\pi\) metres of rope.

Pulling up at one point

Now keep the rope on the ground everywhere except near one point, which you lift until the rope is taut. The rope leaves the sphere along two tangent lines that touch it at angle \(\theta\) either side of the peak. Each tangent has length \(R\tan\theta\), and it replaces an arc of length \(R\theta\). The extra rope used is therefore

$$\Delta L = 2R(\tan\theta - \theta), \qquad h = R\left(\sec\theta - 1\right).$$

For small angles \(\tan\theta - \theta \approx \theta^3/3\) and \(\sec\theta - 1 \approx \theta^2/2\), so

$$\theta \approx \left(\frac{3\,\Delta L}{2R}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad h \approx \frac{R}{2}\left(\frac{3\,\Delta L}{2R}\right)^{2/3} \propto R^{1/3}\,\Delta L^{2/3}.$$

For the Earth (\(R = 6{,}371\text{ km}\)) and one metre of rope, \(\theta \approx 0.35^\circ\) and \(h \approx 121\text{ m}\): taller than the Statue of Liberty. This time the radius does matter, but only as a cube root. The lab solves the exact equation numerically rather than using the approximation.

Why it’s veridical

Nothing in the argument is fishy. Both results are straightforward geometry, and you can check them with a string and a ball. The surprise is entirely in our intuition, which reasons about the proportion \(\Delta L / C\) instead of the absolute change.
