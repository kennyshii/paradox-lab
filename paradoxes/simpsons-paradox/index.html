01 The paradox

A 1986 study compared two treatments for kidney stones. For small stones, treatment A had the higher success rate. For large stones, treatment A had the higher success rate again. Yet when the two groups were combined, treatment B came out ahead overall. How can something be better in every group and worse in total? Surely an average of “better” and “better” is “better”?

02 Make a guess

What explains the reversal?

Doctors gave treatment A mostly to the hard cases (large stones). A’s overall rate is dragged down by its difficult workload, not by being worse.

    04 Why it works

    Overall rates are weighted averages

    An arm’s overall rate is the average of its group rates, weighted by how many of its cases fall in each group:

    $$r_{\text{overall}} = \sum_g w_g\, r_g, \qquad w_g = \frac{n_g}{\sum_h n_h}.$$

    Treatment A beats B within each group (\(93\% > 87\%\) and \(73\% > 69\%\)). But 75% of A’s patients had large stones, versus 23% of B’s:

    $$r_A = 0.25 \times 0.93 + 0.75 \times 0.73 \approx 0.78, \qquad r_B = 0.77 \times 0.87 + 0.23 \times 0.69 \approx 0.83.$$

    The weights differ, so “better in every group” doesn’t carry over. The group (stone size) is a confounder: it affects both which treatment you get and how likely you are to succeed.

    The fair comparison

    Giving both arms the same mix of cases (direct standardisation) removes the confounding and restores A’s advantage. Tick “Compare fairly” in the lab to see it.

    Berkeley

    In 1973, men were admitted at 44% and women at 30%. Yet within departments, women did as well or better in most cases: women had applied mostly to highly competitive departments with low admission rates for everyone. The aggregate gap reflected where people applied, which raises its own questions, rather than departments favouring men.

    Why it’s veridical

    The arithmetic is correct and the data are real. The lesson is practical: which table is right, the aggregated or the split one, depends on the causal story. Here the split table answers “which treatment is better for a given patient?”, but there are other datasets where aggregating is correct.
