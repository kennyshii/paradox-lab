01 The paradox

In 1924 Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski proved that a solid ball in three-dimensional space can be split into finitely many pieces which, moved only by rotations and translations (no stretching, no gaps, no overlaps), reassemble into two solid balls, each identical to the original. Volume seems to have doubled out of nothing. It sounds like a mistake, or a magic trick. Is it?

02 Make a guess

What’s the status of the Banach–Tarski paradox?

It’s a theorem of standard mathematics (ZFC). The pieces are non-measurable: infinitely scattered clouds of points with no well-defined volume, so “volume doubled” never actually happens.

The heart of the proof is not about balls at all but about the free group \(F_2\): every finite sequence of moves \(a, a^{-1}, b, b^{-1}\) with no move immediately undone. Drawn as a tree, each word is a point; four branches leave the centre (the empty word \(e\)), and each branch splits into three. This picture is an explanation aid. You can’t run Banach–Tarski on a computer, because the real pieces can’t be constructed explicitly.

    04 Why it works

    1. A group that duplicates itself

    Split \(F_2\) by first letter into \(W(a), W(a^{-1}), W(b), W(b^{-1})\), plus the identity \(e\). Applying \(a^{-1}\) to every word in \(W(a)\) strips off the leading \(a\), and what remains is every word that doesn’t start with \(a^{-1}\):

    $$a^{-1}W(a) = W(a) \cup W(b) \cup W(b^{-1}) \cup \{e\}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad F_2 = a^{-1}W(a) \;\sqcup\; W(a^{-1}).$$

    Two of the four pieces rebuild all of \(F_2\). By the same trick \(F_2 = b^{-1}W(b) \sqcup W(b^{-1})\): the other two pieces build a second copy. That’s the lab’s diagram.

    2. Put the group inside rotations

    There are two rotations of 3D space, for instance by \(\arccos\tfrac13\) about two perpendicular axes, that behave exactly like \(a\) and \(b\): no non-trivial sequence of them gets you back where you started. So \(F_2\) lives inside the rotation group \(SO(3)\).

    3. Use the axiom of choice to transfer it to the sphere

    These rotations sort the points of the sphere into countably infinite orbits. The axiom of choice lets us pick one representative point from each of the uncountably many orbits, even though no rule can say which. Every point is then “representative × some word”, so the paradoxical split of \(F_2\) becomes a split of the sphere into four pieces, two of which rotate into a whole sphere and two into another. (A countable set of fixed points needs separate handling.) Extending from the sphere to the solid ball, by drawing radii and dealing with the centre, gives the theorem.

    4. Why volume isn’t violated

    Volume is additive only for measurable sets. The pieces built with the axiom of choice are non-measurable, so there is no such thing as “the volume of piece 3”. The argument that volume must be conserved simply doesn’t apply to them. In two dimensions this can’t happen at all, because the rotation group of the plane contains no free group.

    Why it’s veridical

    It is a correct theorem of ZFC, the standard foundation of mathematics, and it has been formally verified with proof assistants. What it really shows is that the axiom of choice produces sets far stranger than anything physical. No one can perform it with a real orange, because real objects are made of finitely many atoms and can’t be cut into non-measurable pieces.
