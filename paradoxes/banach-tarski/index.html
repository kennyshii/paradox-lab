01 The paradox

In 1924 Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski proved that a solid ball in three-dimensional space can be split into finitely many pieces which, moved only by rotations and translations (no stretching, no gaps, no overlaps), reassemble into two solid balls, each identical to the original. Volume seems to have doubled out of nothing. It sounds like a mistake, or a magic trick. Is it?

02 Make a guess

What’s the status of the Banach–Tarski paradox?

It’s a proven theorem. The pieces are infinitely scattered clouds of points, so strange that they don’t have a volume at all. Nothing that has a size ever doubles.

An artist’s impression. The real pieces are so scattered (“non-measurable”) that they have no volume at all, which is why moving them rigidly can double the total. Plays on its own; pick a stage to pause on it.

The trick behind it, in miniature. Start at the centre and take steps right, left, up or down, never undoing the step you just took. Every possible route is a dot on this endless tree. Sort the routes by their first step and you get four pieces. Now watch: just two of those pieces can be shifted to rebuild the whole tree, and the other two can rebuild it again. The real proof does this with rotations of a ball.

    04 Why it works

    • 🧩

      Pieces with no size

      The five pieces are infinitely scattered dust. They’re so strange that it makes no sense to ask how much space they take up.

    • 🏨

      Infinity makes room

      Like the infinite hotel, a ball has infinitely many points, and shuffling infinitely many things around can make room for more.

    • 🪙

      Not for real gold

      Real objects are made of a finite number of atoms, which can’t be cut this way. It’s a true statement about mathematical balls only.
